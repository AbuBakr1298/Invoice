Managing Multiple Currencies in Global Contracts

Updated October 21, 2026 · 3 min read

Billing a client in another country adds three questions to every project: which currency, which exchange rate, and who pays the bank fees. Answer them in the contract and the invoices stay simple. Leave them open and you may find the amount that lands in your account is not the amount you expected.

This guide covers the practical side for freelancers and small businesses billing in US dollars, euros, pounds, Pakistani rupees, Indian rupees or UAE dirhams, the six currencies the QuickInvoice generator supports.

Decide the invoice currency in the contract

Before the first invoice, agree which currency you will bill in and write it into the contract or statement of work. The choice decides who carries exchange-rate risk.

Show the currency code on every amount

Symbols are ambiguous. “$” is used in the United States, Canada, Australia and elsewhere, and “Rs.” is used for both Pakistani and Indian rupees. Add the ISO code, such as USD, EUR, GBP, PKR, INR or AED, next to the total or in your notes. In QuickInvoice you can add a line such as “All amounts in USD” to the Notes & Terms field.

Agree how exchange rates are handled

If the contract price is in one currency but you invoice in another, write down the conversion method. Common approaches are:

  • a fixed rate agreed in the contract;
  • the rate on the invoice date from a named source, such as a central bank;
  • no conversion at all, billing in the contract currency throughout.

QuickInvoice does not convert currencies, so do the conversion first and enter the final amounts in the invoice currency. Do not invent a rate on the invoice. State the rate and its source in the notes so both sides can check it.

Plan for bank charges and fees

International transfers can arrive short. The sending bank, an intermediary bank and the receiving bank may each take a fee, and payment providers add an exchange-rate margin. Say who bears these costs. For international bank transfers the sender can often choose how charges are split (commonly labelled OUR, SHA or BEN), so a line such as “Invoice amounts are payable in full; the payer covers transfer charges” avoids a short payment, provided you have agreed it with the client.

Include the right bank details for international payments

Domestic details are often not enough. Depending on the countries involved, a foreign payer may need your IBAN, the bank’s SWIFT/BIC code, a sort code or routing number, and the bank’s name and address. Ask your bank which details an overseas payer needs and put them on the invoice.

Keep rounding consistent

Most currencies use two decimal places. Round each line to the cent and add the rounded lines, so the figures printed on the invoice add up exactly. QuickInvoice does this automatically.

Remember tax and record-keeping

Whether you charge VAT, GST or sales tax on an international invoice depends on the countries involved and on your registration, so check with an accountant. For your own books, keep the invoice in its original currency and record the converted amount your tax authority asks for.

Quick checklist for international invoices

  • Invoice currency written into the contract.
  • Currency code shown on the invoice, not just a symbol.
  • Exchange-rate method and source agreed in writing.
  • Who pays bank charges agreed and stated.
  • IBAN, SWIFT/BIC or other required bank details included.
