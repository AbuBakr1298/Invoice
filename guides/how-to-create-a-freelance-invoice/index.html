How to Create a Professional Freelance Invoice, Step by Step

Updated October 21, 2026 · 5 min read

A freelance invoice does two jobs. It asks your client for money, and it becomes the record you and your client rely on later for bookkeeping, tax and any question about what was agreed. An invoice that is complete and easy to approve gets paid sooner than one that raises questions.

This guide walks through each part of an invoice in the order it appears on the page, then gives you a checklist to run before you send. What every freelance invoice should include

  • The word “Invoice” and a unique invoice number.
  • Your details: business or trading name, address, email and phone. Add a tax or registration number if your country requires one.
  • Your client’s details: the legal company name and billing address, plus a purchase order or project reference if they use one.
  • Dates: the issue date and the payment due date.
  • Line items: a clear description, quantity and rate for every service or product.
  • Totals: subtotal, any discount, tax, and the total due, in a stated currency.
  • Payment details: how and where to pay, including the reference to use.
  • Terms and notes: payment terms, any late-payment terms you agreed in advance, and a thank-you.

Requirements differ by country. Some tax authorities require specific fields on a valid tax invoice, so check the rules where you operate, or ask an accountant, before relying on any template.

Step 1: Add your details and your client’s

Use the business name that appears on your contract. If you work as a sole trader or freelancer, that is usually your own name or your registered trading name. Add an email address and phone number your client’s accounts team can actually use, and your logo if you have one: it makes the invoice recognisable at a glance.

For the client, copy the company name and billing address exactly as they gave them to you. Accounts departments often match invoices against purchase orders or supplier records, and a small mismatch can hold up approval. If your client gave you a PO number or project code, put it on the invoice.

Step 2: Number and date the invoice

Give every invoice a unique number and never reuse one. A simple running sequence such as INV-0001, INV-0002 works well, and some freelancers add the year, for example 2026-014. Sequential numbers make your records easier to audit and make a missing invoice obvious.

The issue date is the day you send the invoice. The due date follows from your payment terms: with Net 14 terms, an invoice issued on 1 November is due on 15 November. If you are not sure which terms to use, read our guide to Net 7, Net 14 and Net 30 payment terms.

Step 3: Describe the work so it can be approved without questions

Each line should tell the client what they are paying for, how much of it, and at what rate. Specific lines get approved faster because the person paying does not need to ask what “design work” meant.

VagueClear
Design workBrand identity: 3 logo concepts, 2 revision rounds, final files
DevelopmentWebsite development: 24 hours at $60/hour (sprint 3)
ConsultingStrategy workshop: 1 day at $450/day, 12 September

Use whichever unit matches how you priced the job: hours, days, items, or a single fixed fee with a quantity of 1.

Step 4: Show the totals clearly

The usual order is subtotal, then discount, then tax, then the total due. Apply a discount first and calculate tax on the discounted amount; our guide to calculating invoice tax and discounts has a worked example. State the currency on the invoice so there is no doubt what the numbers mean, especially when you bill clients abroad (see invoicing in multiple currencies).

Step 5: Make it easy to pay

Tell the client exactly how to pay. For a bank transfer, include the account name, bank name, account number or IBAN and, for international payments, the SWIFT/BIC code. Ask the client to quote the invoice number as the payment reference so you can match the payment when it arrives. If you accept payment through a link or a QR code, include it; QuickInvoice lets you add a QR code image from a URL.

Double-check every digit. A typo in an account number can send your money somewhere else.

Step 6: State your terms and add a short note

Write the payment terms in plain words, for example “Payment due within 14 days of the invoice date.” If you charge late fees or interest, they need to be agreed in your contract or terms beforehand and must respect local law, so mention them on the invoice only if that is the case. End with a short thank-you; a friendly note costs nothing.

Step 7: Export as a PDF and send it to the right person

A PDF keeps your layout intact on every device and is hard to change by accident. Name the file clearly, for example INV-0007-Apex-Solutions.pdf, and email it to the person who approves or processes payments, not only your day-to-day contact. Put the invoice number and due date in the subject line, such as “Invoice INV-0007, due 15 November”.

Checklist before you send

  • The invoice number is new and follows your sequence.
  • Your details and your client’s details are correct and complete.
  • Every line says what was delivered, how much, and at what rate.
  • The maths adds up and the currency is stated.
  • Tax is applied only where you are required to charge it.
  • The due date matches the terms you agreed.
  • Bank details are correct and a payment reference is given.
  • The file is a PDF with a clear name and is going to the right inbox.
