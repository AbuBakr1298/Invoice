Top Invoicing Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Updated October 21, 2026 · 3 min read

Most late payments are not caused by unwilling clients. They come from invoices that raise questions, reach the wrong person or contain errors that send them back for correction. Each mistake below is easy to fix once you know to look for it.

1. Vague line descriptions

“Services rendered” tells the person approving the invoice nothing, so they have to ask you or dig out the contract. Fix: describe the deliverable, the quantity and the rate on every line, for example “Landing page design, 2 revision rounds, fixed fee”. The freelance invoice guide has before-and-after examples.

2. Wrong or incomplete client details

Companies often have a legal name, a separate billing entity and a department that processes invoices. Bill the wrong entity or leave out a purchase order number and the invoice may be rejected. Fix: at the start of the project, ask for the exact legal name, billing address, PO number and the accounts contact, and copy them exactly.

3. No due date or unclear payment terms

An invoice without a due date has no deadline, so it can sit in a queue for weeks. Fix: put a specific due date on every invoice and agree the terms, such as Net 14, before work begins. Our guide to invoice payment terms explains how to choose.

4. Missing or unclear payment instructions

If the client has to email you for your bank details, you have added a delay to your own payment. Fix: include the account name, bank, account number or IBAN, the SWIFT/BIC code for international payments, and the payment reference to use. Check every digit.

5. Maths errors, especially with tax and discounts

Totals that do not add up are one of the fastest ways to get an invoice sent back. A common slip is calculating tax before the discount, which overcharges tax on money the client never pays. Fix: apply the discount first, then calculate tax on the reduced amount, and let a calculator handle the rounding. See how to calculate invoice tax and discounts.

6. Inconsistent or duplicate invoice numbers

Reused numbers confuse your records and your client’s, and gaps in the sequence make accountants ask questions. Fix: use one running sequence (INV-0001, INV-0002 and so on) and keep a simple log of what you issued.

7. An unclear currency

A bare “$” might mean US, Canadian or Australian dollars, and “Rs.” is used for more than one currency. Fix: state the currency code (USD, EUR, GBP, PKR, INR, AED) on the invoice and in your contract. More in our guide to invoicing in multiple currencies.

8. Invoicing late

The longer you wait, the less your client remembers about the work, and the later you are paid, because payment terms usually run from the invoice date. Fix: invoice as soon as the work is delivered or a milestone is approved. On long projects, invoice in stages instead of at the end.

9. Sending it to the wrong person, then not following up

An invoice sent to a general inbox may never reach the person who approves payment, and silence after sending is not a strategy. Fix: confirm who approves invoices, send it to them, and follow up politely a few days before the due date, on the due date, and shortly after if it is still unpaid.

10. Not keeping records

You will need copies of your invoices for bookkeeping, tax returns and any dispute. Fix: save every PDF in one organised folder or in your accounting software and note the date each was paid. How long you must keep them varies by country, so check the rule that applies to you.

A simple routine that prevents most of these

  1. Before you send: check the client details, the line descriptions and the maths.
  2. When you send: put the invoice number and due date in the email subject, and address it to the person who approves payment.
  3. After you send: note the due date in your calendar and follow up on schedule.
